Who ends up in hospital after a road crash?

People hospitalised after road crashes in Australia, 2011 to 2021. Five questions, one at a time.

  1. National trend
  2. Road users
  3. Age and sex
  4. States and territories
  5. First Nations people

Question 1

How did national counts change from 2011 to 2021, and what happened around 2020?

Road crash hospitalisations rose from 34,033 in 2011 to 39,866 in 2019, an increase of 17.1%. They fell 4.8% to 37,966 in 2020, then recovered to 39,505 in 2021.

Hospitalisations per year, Australia

Source: BITRE hospitalised injury data, cleaned in KNIME (national_annual.csv, national_monthly.csv).

The sharpest monthly drop came in April 2020, with 2,357 hospitalisations, 32.4% below April 2019. This dip coincides with the first COVID-19 restrictions, when far fewer people were travelling. The data shows timing only; it cannot show that the restrictions caused the fall.

Question 2

Which road users drove the change?

Cyclists rose by 2,772 between 2011 and 2021, which is 50.7% of the net rise of 5,472. Their share went from 15.8% to 20.7%. In 2020, cyclists rose 14.1% while car drivers fell 9.4% and pedestrians fell 22.5%.

Question 3

Who is most affected by age and sex?

From 2011 to 2021, ages 65–74 rose 53.4% and ages 17–25 fell 1.0%, against 16.1% overall. Males were 65.3% of 2021 cases. In 2020, females fell 11.8% and males only 0.7%.

Coming in Prototype 2: age-by-year heatmap, plus male and female lines.

These are counts, so differences in population size and age structure explain part of the gaps.

Question 4

How do state and territory trends differ, and what do raw counts hide?

Queensland rose 65.2% from 2011 to 2021. Victoria rose 37.4% from 2012. NSW fell from 10,618 to 9,003, but a change in definition affects that comparison.

Question 5

How do First Nations patterns compare, within the data’s limits?

First Nations hospitalisations rose 90.2%, against 14.9% for non-Indigenous Australians. In 2020 they rose 23.4% while non-Indigenous counts fell 6.1%. In 2021, 23.0% lived in remote areas, against 1.7%.

Coming in Prototype 2: indexed lines (2011 = 100), plus a remoteness bar chart.

These are counts. Population size, age structure and better recording of First Nations status over time may all contribute to the differences.
